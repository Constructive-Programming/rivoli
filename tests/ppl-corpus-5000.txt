The transformer architecture processes a sentence by first converting each token into a
dense vector, then repeatedly mixing those vectors with attention and reshaping them with
feed-forward layers. Attention lets every position look at every other position and decide,
per head, which ones matter. Because the mixing is a weighted sum rather than a recurrence,
all positions are processed in parallel, which is the property that made these models
practical to train at scale.

A mixture-of-experts layer replaces the single feed-forward block with many smaller ones and
a router that selects a handful per token. The parameter count grows without the per-token
compute growing with it, since only the selected experts run. The router is a small linear
layer whose logits are turned into scores; the highest-scoring experts are chosen and their
outputs are combined using those same scores as weights. Load balancing is usually
encouraged with a bias term that nudges the ranking away from collapsing onto a few popular
experts.

Quantization reduces the memory each expert occupies. Vector quantization replaces groups of
weights with indices into a learned codebook, so a block of values costs a few bits each
plus one scale. Integer quantization instead stores a small integer per weight together with
a per-row or per-block scale factor. The first is smaller but its dot product becomes a
random gather, which is limited by cache behaviour rather than arithmetic; the second is
larger but decodes sequentially and runs faster per element.

On a machine where the weights do not all fit in memory at once, the experts must be
streamed from storage as they are selected. A cache holds the most useful subset. When a
requested expert is absent the engine stalls on a read of many megabytes, and the cost of
that stall dominates everything else the layer does. This is why the policy governing which
experts stay resident matters as much as the arithmetic, and why a policy that changes which
experts are selected in the first place is worth measuring carefully.

Positional information has to be injected deliberately, because attention itself is
permutation invariant: shuffle the tokens and the weighted sums come out the same. Early
models added a fixed sinusoidal vector to each embedding. Later work rotates the query and
key vectors by an angle proportional to position, so that the dot product between two
positions depends only on their separation. That relative property is what lets a model
trained on short sequences behave sensibly on longer ones, at least for a while.

Multi-head attention runs several attention operations side by side on slices of the hidden
vector, then concatenates the results. The usual explanation is that different heads
specialise, one tracking syntax, another coreference. The honest version is that heads are
mostly redundant and many can be pruned with little loss, but the redundancy is cheap and
appears to help optimisation, so the design persists largely unchanged from the original.

Normalisation layers keep activations in a workable range as depth grows. Placing them
before each sublayer rather than after makes very deep stacks trainable without a careful
learning-rate warmup, because the residual path stays clean from input to output. The
variant most models now use dispenses with the mean subtraction entirely and rescales by the
root mean square alone, which costs less and appears to lose nothing measurable.

Routing decisions are made independently at every layer, so an expert that matters at layer
ten has no particular claim on layer eleven. This is inconvenient for anything that wants to
predict future demand, and it is why cross-layer prediction is harder than it first sounds.
The correlation that does exist comes from the residual stream changing slowly, not from any
structural link between one layer's router and the next one's.

A cache that holds a fixed number of items must decide what to discard. Recency is the
cheapest signal and works well when access patterns are bursty. Frequency survives scanning
better but adapts slowly to genuine change. The schemes that perform best in practice keep
a little of both, usually by maintaining a probationary segment for newcomers and promoting
only on a second access, so that a one-off reference cannot displace something established.

Measuring a cache policy honestly is harder than implementing one. The tempting number is
end-to-end throughput, which moves for many reasons at once, several of them unrelated to
the policy. A trace replay isolates the residency question by holding the access sequence
fixed. It cannot tell you anything about compute, but what it does tell you, it tells you
without the confounds that make throughput comparisons so easy to misread.

Storage bandwidth has improved faster than latency for two decades, which shapes what a
streaming engine should optimise. Issuing more requests in parallel raises throughput until
the queue saturates; it does nothing at all for the time an individual request takes. A
design that is waiting on one read cannot be rescued by adding readers, and the usual
symptom of getting this wrong is a system that looks busy while making no progress.

Suppose the sequence is bounded and monotone. Then it converges, and its limit is the
supremum of its range. To see this, let s be that supremum; for any positive epsilon, s minus
epsilon is not an upper bound, so some term exceeds it. Monotonicity then puts every later
term in the same interval, and since epsilon was arbitrary the terms are eventually within
any neighbourhood of s.

The construction generalises to arbitrary index sets provided the ordering is directed. What
fails without directedness is the ability to choose a single term dominating any two given
ones, and that choice is used twice in the argument above, once for each of the bounds. A
counterexample with an antichain of size two is easy to write down and shows the hypothesis
cannot simply be dropped.

Uniqueness requires a separate argument. Suppose two limits exist and are distinct; take
epsilon to be a third of their separation. Each limit eventually confines the sequence to its
own neighbourhood, and those neighbourhoods are disjoint by construction, so beyond the later
of the two thresholds the sequence would have to lie in both. It does not, and the
contradiction establishes the claim.

Speculative execution in a decoder amortises the fixed cost of a forward pass across several
candidate tokens. A smaller model proposes a short continuation and the large model verifies
all of it in one batched pass, accepting the longest prefix that matches what it would have
produced anyway. The saving is real but bounded by the acceptance rate, and the machinery
only pays for itself once the per-pass overhead is large relative to the per-token work.

The kernel spends most of its time waiting on memory rather than computing. Arithmetic
intensity, the ratio of operations to bytes moved, is the number that predicts this, and for
a batch of one it is close to the worst case: every weight is read once and used once. Any
optimisation that does not either reduce bytes or increase reuse is decoration, however
clever it looks in the source.

Gradient checkpointing trades recomputation for memory during training by discarding
intermediate activations and regenerating them on the backward pass. The saving is roughly
the square root of the depth if the checkpoints are placed evenly, and the cost is one extra
forward pass through each segment. On hardware where memory is the binding constraint this
is close to free; on hardware where it is not, it is a straightforward waste, and the choice
should be made per configuration rather than adopted as a default.

The attention mask determines what each position is permitted to see. Causal masking forbids
looking forward, which is what makes a single forward pass equivalent to predicting every
position at once. Variants relax this: some allow a fixed window into the past, some allow
attention to a handful of designated sink positions regardless of distance, and some combine
both. The choice interacts with how the cache is stored, since anything that can never be
attended to again can be discarded.

Key-value caching is what makes generation tractable. Without it each new token would
recompute attention over the whole prefix from scratch, quadratic in the length of the
output. With it, the per-token cost is linear in context and the cache grows by one entry
per layer per step. The cache quickly becomes the dominant memory consumer at long context,
which is why it is so often stored at lower precision than the weights that produced it.

Grouped-query attention shares key and value projections across several query heads,
shrinking the cache by the sharing factor at some cost in expressiveness. Multi-head latent
attention goes further, projecting keys and values into a compressed latent space and
reconstructing them on demand, so the cache stores the latent rather than the full vectors.
The reconstruction can often be folded into the surrounding projections, which makes the
saving nearly free at inference time.

An expert that is never selected contributes nothing but still occupies memory. Over a long
run the distribution of selections is far from uniform: a minority of experts account for a
large share of the traffic, and a long tail is touched only a handful of times. This is what
makes caching viable at all, and it is also what makes the tail so expensive, since each
rare selection costs a full read for a single use.

The router's bias term exists to counteract that concentration during training, pushing
probability mass toward under-used experts so that capacity is not wasted. At inference the
bias is frozen, so the balancing pressure it encodes reflects the training distribution
rather than the current input. Any scheme that reorders selections is therefore intervening
on top of an existing intervention, which is worth remembering when interpreting what a
change to the ranking actually does.

Auxiliary losses for load balancing have largely given way to bias-based schemes, partly
because the loss term competes with the language modelling objective and partly because it
is difficult to tune. The bias approach adjusts a per-expert offset based on recent
utilisation, nudging the ranking without adding a term to the gradient. The effect on final
quality appears to be similar and the training dynamics are less delicate.

Calibration data matters more for quantization than the algorithm often does. A few hundred
sequences drawn from the target distribution are usually enough to fit scales that hold up,
while scales fit on mismatched text can be badly wrong in the tails. The failure mode is
subtle: aggregate error looks acceptable while a small number of large-magnitude weights are
clipped, and those are exactly the weights that carry the most signal.

Per-tensor scaling is the crudest option and is rarely adequate below eight bits. Per-row
scaling adapts to the fact that different output channels have different dynamic ranges.
Group-wise scaling subdivides further, storing one scale per contiguous block of weights
along the reduction axis, which costs a little more memory and captures most of the
remaining error. The overhead is a fraction of a bit per weight once the group is reasonably
sized.

Outlier features complicate everything. In large models a handful of hidden dimensions carry
values orders of magnitude larger than the rest, and quantizing them together with their
neighbours destroys the smaller values entirely. Approaches split the difference: keep the
outlier dimensions in higher precision, or rotate the representation so that the energy is
spread more evenly before quantizing, which trades a matrix multiply for a much better
conditioned distribution.

Error accumulates differently depending on where in the network the quantization is applied.
Attention projections tolerate more error than the feed-forward blocks, and the final output
layer tolerates least of all, since its errors are not attenuated by anything downstream. A
uniform bit-width across the whole model is therefore almost always the wrong allocation,
even though it is the easiest to implement and the easiest to describe.

Direct I/O bypasses the page cache, which is the right choice when the access pattern is
larger than memory and has no reuse the kernel could exploit. It also removes a copy. The
cost is that alignment becomes the caller's problem: offsets and lengths must be multiples
of the device block size, and buffers must be aligned in memory, which usually means the
on-disk layout has to be designed around it rather than adapted afterwards.

Asynchronous submission decouples issuing a read from waiting on it. A ring buffer shared
with the kernel lets many requests be queued with a single system call and completions
harvested the same way. The benefit is not fewer syscalls in themselves but the ability to
keep many reads in flight, which is what saturates a device whose individual latency is high
relative to its throughput.

Overlapping transfer with computation hides latency but creates no bandwidth. If the
computation and the transfer contend for the same memory bus, as they do on a unified-memory
part, then perfect overlap still leaves total time bounded below by total traffic divided by
bus width. This is the distinction between hiding a cost and avoiding one, and conflating
them is the most common error in reasoning about streaming systems.

Eviction and admission are separate decisions that are often conflated. Eviction asks which
resident item to remove; admission asks whether an incoming item deserves to displace
anything at all. A policy that admits everything will thrash under a scanning workload no
matter how good its eviction order is, which is why probationary segments and frequency
filters tend to matter more than the choice between recency orderings.

Let the function be continuous on a closed bounded interval. Then it is uniformly continuous
there. Suppose otherwise: there is a positive epsilon and pairs of points arbitrarily close
together whose images differ by at least epsilon. Extract a convergent subsequence from the
first coordinates; the second coordinates converge to the same limit. Continuity at that
limit contradicts the separation of the images, so no such epsilon exists.

The compactness hypothesis is doing all the work and cannot be dropped. On an open interval
the reciprocal function is continuous but not uniformly so: points near the excluded endpoint
can be arbitrarily close while their images are arbitrarily far apart. The extraction step
above fails precisely because the subsequence's limit is not in the domain, so there is no
point at which to invoke continuity.

An analogous statement holds for functions between metric spaces whenever the domain is
compact, and the proof is unchanged except that convergence is stated in terms of the
metrics. The result fails for merely complete domains, and the standard counterexample is
again a function whose modulus of continuity degrades without bound along a sequence with no
convergent subsequence.

To extend to the several-variable case, note that the argument used only sequential
compactness and the triangle inequality. Both are available in any finite product of closed
bounded intervals, since a product of sequentially compact spaces is sequentially compact for
finite products. The infinite-dimensional case requires more care, and the naive extension is
false without additional hypotheses on the family of functions.
