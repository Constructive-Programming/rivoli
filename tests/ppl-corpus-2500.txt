The transformer architecture processes a sentence by first converting each token into a
dense vector, then repeatedly mixing those vectors with attention and reshaping them with
feed-forward layers. Attention lets every position look at every other position and decide,
per head, which ones matter. Because the mixing is a weighted sum rather than a recurrence,
all positions are processed in parallel, which is the property that made these models
practical to train at scale.

A mixture-of-experts layer replaces the single feed-forward block with many smaller ones and
a router that selects a handful per token. The parameter count grows without the per-token
compute growing with it, since only the selected experts run. The router is a small linear
layer whose logits are turned into scores; the highest-scoring experts are chosen and their
outputs are combined using those same scores as weights. Load balancing is usually
encouraged with a bias term that nudges the ranking away from collapsing onto a few popular
experts.

Quantization reduces the memory each expert occupies. Vector quantization replaces groups of
weights with indices into a learned codebook, so a block of values costs a few bits each
plus one scale. Integer quantization instead stores a small integer per weight together with
a per-row or per-block scale factor. The first is smaller but its dot product becomes a
random gather, which is limited by cache behaviour rather than arithmetic; the second is
larger but decodes sequentially and runs faster per element.

On a machine where the weights do not all fit in memory at once, the experts must be
streamed from storage as they are selected. A cache holds the most useful subset. When a
requested expert is absent the engine stalls on a read of many megabytes, and the cost of
that stall dominates everything else the layer does. This is why the policy governing which
experts stay resident matters as much as the arithmetic, and why a policy that changes which
experts are selected in the first place is worth measuring carefully.

Positional information has to be injected deliberately, because attention itself is
permutation invariant: shuffle the tokens and the weighted sums come out the same. Early
models added a fixed sinusoidal vector to each embedding. Later work rotates the query and
key vectors by an angle proportional to position, so that the dot product between two
positions depends only on their separation. That relative property is what lets a model
trained on short sequences behave sensibly on longer ones, at least for a while.

Multi-head attention runs several attention operations side by side on slices of the hidden
vector, then concatenates the results. The usual explanation is that different heads
specialise, one tracking syntax, another coreference. The honest version is that heads are
mostly redundant and many can be pruned with little loss, but the redundancy is cheap and
appears to help optimisation, so the design persists largely unchanged from the original.

Normalisation layers keep activations in a workable range as depth grows. Placing them
before each sublayer rather than after makes very deep stacks trainable without a careful
learning-rate warmup, because the residual path stays clean from input to output. The
variant most models now use dispenses with the mean subtraction entirely and rescales by the
root mean square alone, which costs less and appears to lose nothing measurable.

Routing decisions are made independently at every layer, so an expert that matters at layer
ten has no particular claim on layer eleven. This is inconvenient for anything that wants to
predict future demand, and it is why cross-layer prediction is harder than it first sounds.
The correlation that does exist comes from the residual stream changing slowly, not from any
structural link between one layer's router and the next one's.

A cache that holds a fixed number of items must decide what to discard. Recency is the
cheapest signal and works well when access patterns are bursty. Frequency survives scanning
better but adapts slowly to genuine change. The schemes that perform best in practice keep
a little of both, usually by maintaining a probationary segment for newcomers and promoting
only on a second access, so that a one-off reference cannot displace something established.

Measuring a cache policy honestly is harder than implementing one. The tempting number is
end-to-end throughput, which moves for many reasons at once, several of them unrelated to
the policy. A trace replay isolates the residency question by holding the access sequence
fixed. It cannot tell you anything about compute, but what it does tell you, it tells you
without the confounds that make throughput comparisons so easy to misread.

Storage bandwidth has improved faster than latency for two decades, which shapes what a
streaming engine should optimise. Issuing more requests in parallel raises throughput until
the queue saturates; it does nothing at all for the time an individual request takes. A
design that is waiting on one read cannot be rescued by adding readers, and the usual
symptom of getting this wrong is a system that looks busy while making no progress.

Suppose the sequence is bounded and monotone. Then it converges, and its limit is the
supremum of its range. To see this, let s be that supremum; for any positive epsilon, s minus
epsilon is not an upper bound, so some term exceeds it. Monotonicity then puts every later
term in the same interval, and since epsilon was arbitrary the terms are eventually within
any neighbourhood of s.

The construction generalises to arbitrary index sets provided the ordering is directed. What
fails without directedness is the ability to choose a single term dominating any two given
ones, and that choice is used twice in the argument above, once for each of the bounds. A
counterexample with an antichain of size two is easy to write down and shows the hypothesis
cannot simply be dropped.

Uniqueness requires a separate argument. Suppose two limits exist and are distinct; take
epsilon to be a third of their separation. Each limit eventually confines the sequence to its
own neighbourhood, and those neighbourhoods are disjoint by construction, so beyond the later
of the two thresholds the sequence would have to lie in both. It does not, and the
contradiction establishes the claim.

Speculative execution in a decoder amortises the fixed cost of a forward pass across several
candidate tokens. A smaller model proposes a short continuation and the large model verifies
all of it in one batched pass, accepting the longest prefix that matches what it would have
produced anyway. The saving is real but bounded by the acceptance rate, and the machinery
only pays for itself once the per-pass overhead is large relative to the per-token work.

The kernel spends most of its time waiting on memory rather than computing. Arithmetic
intensity, the ratio of operations to bytes moved, is the number that predicts this, and for
a batch of one it is close to the worst case: every weight is read once and used once. Any
optimisation that does not either reduce bytes or increase reuse is decoration, however
clever it looks in the source.
